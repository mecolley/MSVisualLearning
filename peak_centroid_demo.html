Finding a peak’s center

The estimators

Apex sample
The m/z of the highest sampled point. Error is spread evenly over half a sampling step either side.
Center of mass
Intensity-weighted mean of the points above half the maximum. Averages noise, but shifts as points enter or leave the window.
Parabola, 3 points
A parabola through the apex and its two neighbors. Cheap, with a small bias because a Gaussian is not a parabola.
Gaussian, 3 points
The same parabola fitted to log intensities. Exact for a noise-free Gaussian; fails when a neighbor is zero.
Gaussian least-squares fit
Height, center and width fitted to all points within 1.5 FWHM. Uses the most information, but needs a starting guess and can fail on noisy data.

Reading the results

RMS is the typical size of the error over 2,000 trials, each with a random sub-sample offset and fresh noise. Bias is the average error; a value near zero means the estimator does not lean in one direction.

With only 2–3 points per FWHM, methods that assume a peak shape do best. With 8 or more points and real noise, the three-point methods lose ground, because three points near the top carry little information, and methods that use many points win.

No estimator can beat the counting limit: a peak built from N ions has a center uncertain by roughly σ/√N, where σ = FWHM / 2.355.
