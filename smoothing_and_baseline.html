Smoothing and baseline removal

Random noise changes from point to point, faster than any real peak. A baseline changes slowly, over many peak widths. Smoothing and baseline removal both rely on that difference in scale. This low-resolution, axial time-of-flight spectrum adds a complication: at low mass, a crowd of peaks too close to resolve forms a raised floor that a baseline method can’t tell apart from a real offset.

The processing

Windows are measured in peak widths at m/z 6,000. On a flight-time axis, low-mass peaks are narrower, so the same window spans more of them.

Try this

  1. With SNIP and a 16-width window, check “Unresolved crowd removed as baseline”: most of the crowd is subtracted as if it were baseline, though it is real signal. Set noise to 0 to see it clearly at the low-mass end of the lower plot.
  2. Widen the baseline window as far as it goes. SNIP keeps treating most of the crowd as baseline at every width, because a smooth hump falling away from the low-mass edge has no peak shape for it to clip. Rolling minimum lets go of some of it only at around 300 peak widths, by which point its error at high mass has grown. No window separates the crowd from a true baseline.
  3. Shrink the baseline window below one peak width. The baseline climbs into the real peaks and takes their height.
  4. With moving average, widen the smoothing window past one peak width. Noise drops, but the isolated peak loses height and the close pair merges. Savitzky–Golay keeps more of the height.
  5. Raise the noise and watch “Baseline pulled down by noise.” Minimum-based methods follow the lowest points, so noise drags the whole estimate below the true baseline.

Key ideas

Every smoothing filter is a trade: it averages away noise narrower than its window and also flattens anything else that narrow, including the tops of peaks and the dip between close peaks. Savitzky–Golay fits a small polynomial at each point, so it follows peak curvature better than a plain average.

A baseline method only sees shape. SNIP removes features that are peaked and keeps anything that changes smoothly, so a crowd of peaks too close to resolve, being broad and smooth, looks like baseline and gets removed with it. Whether that is acceptable depends on the question; the real fix is more resolving power, which turns the crowd back into separate peaks.

A true baseline, an offset not made of resolvable ions, varies slowly. In axial time-of-flight instruments with sparse signal it tends to lift first at high m/z, so the baseline window must be narrow enough to follow that rise and wide enough to pass under real peaks.

Assumptions in this simulation

A linear (axial) TOF at resolving power 600, sampled every 4 ns over flight times of about 22–52 µs. Peaks are Gaussian in flight time and noise is Gaussian. The shape of the high-mass lift is illustrative, a smooth rise, not a model of a particular mechanism, and the unresolved crowd stands for any dense set of small peaks.

Where these ideas come from

The original papers and books behind the concepts in this lesson.

  • Savitzky–Golay smoothing

    Savitzky, A. & Golay, M. J. E. (1964). Smoothing and differentiation of data by simplified least squares procedures. Analytical Chemistry 36(8), 1627–1639.

    Steinier, J., Termonia, Y. & Deltour, J. (1972). Comments on smoothing and differentiation of data by simplified least square procedure. Analytical Chemistry 44(11), 1906–1909.

  • Matched filtering

    Turin, G. L. (1960). An introduction to matched filters. IRE Transactions on Information Theory 6(3), 311–329.

  • SNIP baseline estimation

    Ryan, C. G., Clayton, E., Griffin, W. L., Sie, S. H. & Cousens, D. R. (1988). SNIP, a statistics-sensitive background treatment for the quantitative analysis of PIXE spectra in geoscience applications. Nuclear Instruments and Methods in Physics Research B 34(3), 396–402.

    Morháč, M. & Matoušek, V. (2008). Peak clipping algorithms for background estimation in spectroscopic data. Applied Spectroscopy 62(1), 91–106.

  • Rolling minimum as a morphological opening

    Serra, J. (1982). Image Analysis and Mathematical Morphology. Academic Press.

  • MALDI and linear time-of-flight analysis

    Karas, M. & Hillenkamp, F. (1988). Laser desorption ionization of proteins with molecular masses exceeding 10,000 daltons. Analytical Chemistry 60(20), 2299–2301.

    Tanaka, K., Waki, H., Ido, Y., Akita, S., Yoshida, Y. & Yoshida, T. (1988). Protein and polymer analyses up to m/z 100 000 by laser ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Rapid Communications in Mass Spectrometry 2(8), 151–153.

    Wiley, W. C. & McLaren, I. H. (1955). Time-of-flight mass spectrometer with improved resolution. Review of Scientific Instruments 26(12), 1150–1157.

  • Smoothing and baseline removal in MALDI-TOF software

    Gibb, S. & Strimmer, K. (2012). MALDIquant: a versatile R package for the analysis of mass spectrometry data. Bioinformatics 28(17), 2270–2271.
