What noise looks like

“Noise” covers several different things. Some is random and shrinks when you average scans; some is real chemistry that repeats in every scan and never averages away. Build a spectrum from each source and average up to 256 scans to see the difference.

Try this

  1. Set chemical background to 0. Both sets of dots now follow the dashed 1 ÷ √N line: all remaining noise is random.
  2. Raise chemical background. The orange dots level off; averaging more scans stops helping, because those peaks are real.
  3. Set electronic noise to 0 and ions to 10. What’s left is pure counting noise, which is lumpy and never negative.
  4. Turn on the digitizer threshold with a small baseline. Most points become exactly zero, and the average of thresholded scans sits below the true level.
  5. Set electronic noise to 0 and watch “Variance ÷ mean.” With ideal counting it sits near 1. Switch to the analog detector and it rises to about 1 + c²: the pulse-height spread adds noise on top of counting noise.

Key ideas

Counting (shot) noise comes from ions arriving one at a time. A bin expecting λ counts varies by √λ, so noise grows with signal and a single “noise level” doesn’t fit a whole spectrum.

Electronic noise is present even with no ions and is roughly constant. Digitizers often cut it off with a threshold, which is why many TOF spectra are mostly zeros.

Random noise of any kind averages down as 1 ÷ √N over N scans. Chemical background and baseline repeat in every scan, so averaging makes them clearer, not smaller.

What looks like a raised baseline is often a crowd of small peaks too close together to resolve. It shrinks as resolving power increases, which is why this high-resolution window shows most of the chemical background as separate peaks. A true baseline, an offset not made of resolvable ions, varies slowly; in axial time-of-flight instruments with sparse signal, it tends to lift first at high m/z.

For counting noise, a signal’s variance grows in proportion to its mean. On real data, plotting variance against mean for many peaks across repeated scans gives a slope that converts arbitrary intensity units into “effective ions,” including the extra noise from variable pulse heights.

Assumptions in this simulation

The unresolved background is flat across the window. Each bin is independent: the analog detector’s pulses add random height but not width here (the anatomy lesson shows pulse width). Ion numbers are a teaching range, not values from a particular instrument, and this is a time-of-flight picture; Orbitrap and FT-ICR noise comes mainly from the electronics measuring the image current.

Where these ideas come from

The original papers and books behind the concepts in this lesson.

  • Counting statistics

    Poisson, S. D. (1837). Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements en matière criminelle et en matière civile. Paris: Bachelier.

  • Shot noise from discrete charges

    Schottky, W. (1918). Über spontane Stromschwankungen in verschiedenen Elektrizitätsleitern. Annalen der Physik 362(23), 541–567.

  • Thermal (electronic) noise

    Johnson, J. B. (1928). Thermal agitation of electricity in conductors. Physical Review 32(1), 97–109.

    Nyquist, H. (1928). Thermal agitation of electric charge in conductors. Physical Review 32(1), 110–113.

  • Excess noise from variable detector gain

    Shockley, W. & Pierce, J. R. (1938). A theory of noise for electron multipliers. Proceedings of the Institute of Radio Engineers 26(3), 321–332.

    Wiza, J. L. (1979). Microchannel plate detectors. Nuclear Instruments and Methods 162(1–3), 587–601.

  • Averaging and how uncertainty falls as 1 ÷ √N

    Bevington, P. R. & Robinson, D. K. (2003). Data Reduction and Error Analysis for the Physical Sciences, 3rd edition. McGraw-Hill.

  • Calibrating signal units from variance versus mean (the photon transfer method)

    Janesick, J. R. (2007). Photon Transfer: DN → λ. SPIE Press.

  • Resolving power and what high resolution separates

    Marshall, A. G. & Hendrickson, C. L. (2008). High-resolution mass spectrometers. Annual Review of Analytical Chemistry 1, 579–599.
