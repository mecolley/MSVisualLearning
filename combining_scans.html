Combining many scans

Most measurements repeat the same scan many times. You can add the scans together and then find peaks, or find peaks in each scan and then combine them. Each approach has a weakness, and drift between scans exposes both.

Each scan has a strong peak (40 × noise) and a weak one. Peaks are 25 ppm wide, sampled at 6 points per width; a peak counts as found when it reaches 3 × the noise.

Try this

  1. Set drift to none and increase the scans. The weak peak, invisible in one scan, rises out of the noise in the average: signal-to-noise grows as √N.
  2. Now raise the steady drift to 60–80 ppm. The averaged strong peak gets wider and shorter, and the weak peak’s signal-to-noise falls with it, throwing away much of what averaging gained.
  3. Turn on the lock mass correction. Each scan is shifted back using the strong peak, and the average is sharp again.
  4. Compare “weak peak found in” with the averaged result at low signal-to-noise. Picking first misses weak peaks that summing would find.

Key ideas

Summing first gives the best sensitivity, because random noise averages down while the signal adds up. But any shift between scans smears the summed peak, costing both height and position accuracy.

Picking first is immune to smearing and shows the drift directly, since each scan’s centroid is measured on its own. But a peak must be detectable in individual scans to be picked at all.

A common practice combines both: correct each scan’s m/z with a lock mass, sum to find the peaks, then measure each peak in every scan.

Where these ideas come from

The original papers and books behind the concepts in this lesson.

  • Averaging and how uncertainty falls as 1 ÷ √N

    Bevington, P. R. & Robinson, D. K. (2003). Data Reduction and Error Analysis for the Physical Sciences, 3rd edition. McGraw-Hill.

  • Lock masses for correcting drift

    Olsen, J. V., de Godoy, L. M. F., Li, G., Macek, B., Mortensen, P., Pesch, R., Makarov, A., Lange, O., Horning, S. & Mann, M. (2005). Parts per million mass accuracy on an Orbitrap mass spectrometer via lock mass injection into a C-trap. Molecular & Cellular Proteomics 4(12), 2010–2021.

  • Aligning and matching peaks across many runs

    Smith, C. A., Want, E. J., O’Maille, G., Abagyan, R. & Siuzdak, G. (2006). XCMS: processing mass spectrometry data for metabolite profiling using nonlinear peak alignment, matching, and identification. Analytical Chemistry 78(3), 779–787.

  • Warping to correct shifts between spectra

    Eilers, P. H. C. (2004). Parametric time warping. Analytical Chemistry 76(2), 404–411.

    Bloemberg, T. G., Gerretzen, J., Wouters, H. J. P., Gloerich, J., et al. (2010). Improved parametric time warping for proteomics. Chemometrics and Intelligent Laboratory Systems 104, 65–74.

    Wehrens, R., Bloemberg, T. G. & Eilers, P. H. C. (2015). Fast parametric warping of peak lists. Bioinformatics 31, 3063–3065.

  • Mass alignment across many spectra in imaging

    Eriksson, J. O., Sánchez Brotons, A., Rezeli, M., Suits, F., Markó-Varga, G. & Horvatovich, P. (2020). MSIWarp: a general approach to mass alignment in mass spectrometry imaging. Analytical Chemistry 92(24), 16138–16148.
