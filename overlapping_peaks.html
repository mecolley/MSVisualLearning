Overlapping peaks and resolution

Two ions closer than about one peak width don’t look like two peaks. They look like one slightly wide, slightly lopsided peak, and a peak picker reports one m/z somewhere between them. Move the second peak and watch what the signal, and the software, can and can’t tell you.

Settings

One FWHM is 25 ppm here, about 40,000 resolving power.

Two equal peaks show only one maximum when they are closer than 0.85 FWHM. Even one full FWHM apart, the dip between them is only about 7% below the tops.

Try this

  1. With equal heights, slide separation down from 2 to 0.5 FWHM. Note where the second maximum disappears and how the apparent width grows.
  2. Set B to 0.2 of A at 0.6 FWHM. The pair looks like one clean peak, but its center is pulled toward B, shifting the reported m/z by several ppm.
  3. Watch the two-peak fit at 0.5 FWHM with 1% noise, then 5% noise. Knowing the peak shape can split a pair the eye can’t, until noise makes the answer unstable.

Key ideas

Resolution is about the dip between peaks, and the dip vanishes earlier than most people expect. A peak that is wider than the instrument should produce at that m/z is a strong hint that it contains more than one ion.

A single-peak centroid of an unresolved pair lands near the intensity-weighted mean of the two positions. If their ratio changes from scan to scan or sample to sample, the reported m/z wanders with it.

Fitting several peaks of known shape (deconvolution) can separate pairs closer than the resolution, but it needs good signal-to-noise and an accurate peak model.
