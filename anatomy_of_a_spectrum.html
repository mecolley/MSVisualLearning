Anatomy of a spectrum

Every peak is built from individual ions, each landing at a slightly different spot on the m/z axis. The instrument counts them in narrow bins, which gives the profile you see. Change the number of ions, the resolving power and the sampling to see what each one controls.

Try this

  1. Drag ions from 10 to 100,000. With few ions the profile is a handful of uneven counts; with many it becomes a smooth bell, and the centroid error shrinks.
  2. Change the m/z with resolving power fixed. The width in m/z units changes, but the width in ppm stays the same.
  3. Set sampling to 1–2 points per FWHM. The profile gets blocky, yet with enough ions the centroid can still be accurate.
  4. Press “Draw new ions” several times with 100 ions and watch how much the centroid moves from draw to draw.
  5. Switch the detector to analog and set ions to 10. Each ion is now a pulse of random height spread over several bins, so a few ions look like a lumpy peak rather than isolated spikes. Raise the pulse-height spread and the expected precision gets worse.

Key ideas

A peak is a distribution. Ions of one m/z don’t all arrive at once; their spread is the instrument’s peak shape, and its width is set by resolving power: FWHM = (m/z) ÷ R.

That shape is the probability of where each ion lands, so it doesn’t change with the number of ions; the gray curve stays the same. Fewer ions just means fewer random draws from it, so the measured profile becomes a sparse, uneven sample of the shape rather than a smaller copy of it.

A profile spectrum is that distribution counted in narrow bins. A centroid spectrum replaces each peak with one m/z and one intensity, which is far smaller but throws away the width and shape.

Because each ion is a random draw, a peak’s position is uncertain by about σ ÷ √N, where σ = FWHM ÷ 2.355 and N is the number of ions. Weak peaks are imprecise however they are processed.

Most high-resolution TOF instruments digitize an analog detector signal. Each ion makes a pulse that can be nearly as wide as the peak itself, with a height that varies from ion to ion. That variation acts like losing ions: N ions carry about the statistical weight of N ÷ (1 + c²) ideal counts, where c is the pulse-height coefficient of variation.

Assumptions in this simulation

Peaks are Gaussian and keep the same shape at every intensity. Real TOF peaks often tail, and very intense peaks distort through detector saturation, dead time and space charge, none of which is modeled here.

This is a picture of a time-of-flight detector. Orbitrap and FT-ICR instruments measure an image current rather than counting ions, so their peaks don’t break up like this at low ion numbers; they fade into electronic noise instead.

The ion numbers on the slider are a teaching range, from barely detectable to very precise, not values from a particular instrument.
