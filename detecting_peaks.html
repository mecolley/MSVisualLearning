Detecting peaks

A peak picker has to decide, for every local maximum, whether it is taller than noise alone would make it. This spectrum has 25 real peaks of known position and height in Gaussian noise, so every decision can be scored.

Settings

There are always 25 real peaks; a longer spectrum only adds more noise.

Real peaks have heights between 2 and 30 times the noise. A detection counts as real if it lies within one peak width of a real peak.

Try this

  1. At a threshold of 3, switch length from 2,000 to 200,000 points. The real peaks don’t change, but false peaks multiply: more points means more chances for noise to cross the line.
  2. Choose “Standard deviation of all points.” The real peaks inflate the estimate, raising the bar for everything; MAD is barely affected.
  3. Turn on the digitizer threshold with MAD. Most points become zero, the MAD collapses toward zero, and false peaks explode.
  4. Turn on the matched filter and lower the threshold. Weak real peaks stand out better, so you can find more of them for the same false-peak count.

Key ideas

Detection is a test repeated at every point. Gaussian noise exceeds 3σ at about 0.13% of points, which is roughly 270 points in a 200,000-point spectrum. A threshold that sounds strict still lets junk through in long spectra.

The noise estimate matters as much as the threshold. Robust statistics like the median absolute deviation ignore the peaks, but any statistic of mostly zeros is near zero.

A matched filter (smoothing with the peak’s own shape) is the best linear way to find peaks of known shape in random noise. Height alone is never the only clue; width, repetition across scans and isotope partners help too.
